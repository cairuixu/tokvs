What Is Bitcoin?

No company, no headquarters, no forecasts: just what Bitcoin is, how it works, and what you should know before you buy any.

The short answer

Bitcoin is a decentralized digital currency: money that moves over the internet without banks, payment companies, or any single company in charge. It was launched in 2009, it runs on a public ledger called the blockchain, and its total supply is capped at 21 million coins — a limit written into its code that no one can change unilaterally. People use it to store value, to send money across borders, and, increasingly, as the entry point into the wider crypto market. If you only remember one sentence, remember this: Bitcoin is money with no issuer, kept honest by a network instead of an institution.

Where Bitcoin came from

In October 2008, in the middle of a global financial crisis, a person or group using the pseudonym Satoshi Nakamoto published a short paper titled "Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System." The paper described a way for people to send value directly to each other online without trusting a bank or payment processor to intermediate. In January 2009 the network went live with its first block, and the idea stopped being a paper. Nobody knows who Satoshi was, and that is part of the design rather than a mystery to solve: Bitcoin has no founder to arrest, no CEO to pressure, and no company that can change the rules for its own benefit. Development is open — anyone can read the code, propose changes, and run the software.

That origin story matters because it explains Bitcoin's unusual properties. Traditional money works because you trust the issuer: a central bank manages supply and a banking system moves it around. Bitcoin works because you do not have to trust any single party: the rules are public, the ledger is public, and thousands of independent participants enforce the same rules. If that sounds abstract, the next section makes it concrete.

How Bitcoin actually works

Three pieces do all the work. First, the blockchain: a public ledger, copied on thousands of computers worldwide, that records every Bitcoin transaction ever made. Transactions are grouped into "blocks," and each block links to the one before it, forming a chain. Once a block is buried under enough later blocks, changing it would require rewriting everything after it on a majority of copies — practically impossible, which is why the ledger is treated as final.

Second, mining: the process that adds new blocks and issues new coins. Miners are computers competing to solve a computational puzzle; the winner gets to add the next block and receives newly created bitcoin as a reward. Mining does two jobs at once: it orders transactions honestly (cheating requires out-computing the honest majority, which costs real electricity and hardware) and it controls the release of new coins on a fixed schedule. Third, keys: your bitcoin is controlled by a private key — a long secret number — and your public address is derived from it. Anyone with the private key can spend the coins; anyone without it cannot. That single fact explains both Bitcoin's security and its most famous risk: lose the key, lose the coins, with no password reset.

Why 21 million matters

Bitcoin's code caps total supply at 21 million coins, and new coins enter circulation through mining rewards that shrink over time. Roughly every four years, an event called the halving cuts the mining reward in half, slowing new supply on a schedule anyone can verify in advance. This is the opposite of how fiat money works, where a central bank can expand supply at will. Scarcity alone does not make something valuable — plenty of scarce things are worthless — but a predictable, unchangeable supply is the reason people describe Bitcoin as "digital gold": an asset whose future issuance nobody can quietly inflate.

Two practical consequences follow. One, Bitcoin is divisible: you never need to buy a whole coin. One bitcoin divides into 100 million smaller units called satoshis, so you can own $10 worth of Bitcoin exactly like you own $10 worth of gold dust. Two, lost coins are gone forever — coins whose keys were lost still count toward the 21 million, which means the spendable supply is smaller than the headline number. Neither fact requires you to believe any price forecast; they are mechanics, and mechanics are what you should base decisions on.

That table is also the honest trade-off: Bitcoin wins on censorship resistance, borderlessness, and predictable supply, and loses badly on price stability and consumer protection. A bank transfer can be reversed when you are scammed; a Bitcoin transfer cannot. A bank account is denominated in a currency your landlord accepts; Bitcoin's price can swing sharply in a week. Neither side of that ledger is a reason to buy or avoid Bitcoin on its own — it is the context for deciding how much, if any, belongs in your finances.

How people buy bitcoin

The standard beginner path runs through an exchange: register, verify your identity, buy USDT with fiat money, then use USDT to buy BTC on a spot pair. Our bitcoin walkthrough covers that flow step by step, and our beginner's buying guide covers the same ground for crypto in general. After buying, you choose storage: leave it on the exchange while the amount is small and you are learning, or move it to your own wallet — where you alone hold the private key — once the amount matters. Our wallet safety guide explains seed phrases, the twelve or twenty-four words that back up a self-custody wallet, and the discipline they demand.

What you should not do on day one: buy with leverage, chase a coin because someone online promised returns, or send bitcoin to any address a stranger gave you for any reason — "giveaway," "support," "investment doubling," all of it. Bitcoin's finality is a feature for honest transfers and a weapon for scammers; the defense is boring and absolute: verify addresses yourself, never share keys or codes, and assume every unsolicited offer is a trap until proven otherwise.

How a Bitcoin payment settles

When you send bitcoin, your wallet broadcasts a signed transaction to the network, where it waits in a staging area called the mempool. Miners pick transactions from the mempool — typically preferring those with higher fees when space is tight — and the winning miner includes yours in the next block, roughly ten minutes later. That first block is your first confirmation. Most recipients consider a payment settled after several confirmations, because each additional block buried on top makes reversing the transaction exponentially harder. For small everyday amounts, one confirmation is usually enough; for large amounts, waiting for six is the common convention. None of this requires trusting the recipient or an intermediary — the network's shared rules do the verifying, and your wallet shows the confirmation count as it grows.

Two practical habits follow from that mechanics lesson. First, fees are a market, not a menu: when the mempool is crowded, fees rise, and paying the wallet's suggested fee is usually right for beginners. Second, never treat an unconfirmed transaction as finished money. If a seller releases goods before confirmation and the transaction later fails or is replaced, there is no one to appeal to. Patience is part of the protocol: ten minutes for a block, a few blocks for confidence, and no shortcuts that skip either.

The risks, stated plainly

Volatility. Bitcoin's price has repeatedly fallen by half or more from peaks and taken months or years to recover. Anyone who cannot watch their holding drop sharply without panic-selling should size accordingly or stay out.

Irreversibility. A mistaken address or a scammer's address means the coins are gone. There is no support desk, no chargeback, and no insurance fund for user error.

Self-custody responsibility. Holding your own keys removes exchange risk and replaces it with personal responsibility: back up the seed phrase offline, never photograph it, never type it into a website.

Regulation varies. Rules differ by country and change over time — tax treatment, exchange availability, and even legality. Check your local rules before buying, and keep records of every transaction for tax time.

Scams cluster around Bitcoin's name. Fake exchanges, fake support accounts, fake giveaways, and "recovery services" that charge victims twice all use Bitcoin's fame as bait. Our scam guide lists the eight frauds beginners actually meet.

Myths that won't die

  • "Bitcoin is anonymous." It is pseudonymous: addresses are not names, but every transaction is public forever, and exchanges link addresses to identities. Treat it as traceable, not invisible.
  • "Bitcoin is only for crime." Illicit use exists, as it does for cash, but the overwhelming majority of volume is trading, holding, and legitimate transfers on regulated venues.
  • "Bitcoin is a company that could go bankrupt." There is no company. The network is software run by independent participants; it continues as long as people run it.
  • "It is too late to buy." That is a price opinion disguised as a fact. Whether Bitcoin fits your finances depends on your goals and risk tolerance, not on what year it is.
  • "Bitcoin is backed by nothing." So is every modern fiat currency. Bitcoin is backed by its network security, its fixed supply schedule, and the willingness of people to accept it — the same kind of social backing, minus the issuer.

Who controls Bitcoin?

No one person, company, or government controls Bitcoin. Its rules live in open-source software run by thousands of independent participants; changing the rules requires broad agreement across miners, node operators, and users.

Can Bitcoin be copied or duplicated?

Anyone can copy the software, but a copy does not inherit Bitcoin's network, its ledger history, or its holders. Value comes from the live network everyone actually uses, not from the code alone.

How many bitcoins will ever exist?

21 million, enforced by the code. New coins enter only through mining rewards, which halve roughly every four years, so issuance slows on a schedule anyone can verify.

Do I need to buy a whole bitcoin?

No. Bitcoin divides into 100 million satoshis per coin, so you can buy $10 worth exactly. Most beginners own a fraction of a coin, not a whole one.

Is Bitcoin anonymous?

No — it is pseudonymous. Transactions are public on the blockchain forever, and regulated exchanges link addresses to real identities. Assume your on-chain activity is traceable.

What happens when all 21 million are mined?

Mining rewards end and miners will be paid only from transaction fees. That transition is decades away and is already part of the design; it does not shut the network down.
