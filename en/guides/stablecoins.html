Stablecoins 101: USDT vs USDC

A stablecoin is crypto pegged to the dollar: 1 token ≈ $1. USDT is the largest and most widely accepted; USDC has the stronger reputation for compliance and transparency. For everyday trading both work — the differences matter for large or long-term holdings.

Why crypto needs stablecoins

Coin prices swing hard, so traders need somewhere to pause: sell BTC into a stablecoin and you've locked in dollar value inside the market, without cashing out to a bank. Buying back in takes seconds. Stablecoins are the cash layer of the whole system — which is why most people's first purchase is the stablecoin itself; see How to Buy USDT.

USDT vs USDC, line by line

USDT (Tether)USDC
IssuerTetherCircle
ScaleLargest stablecoin by a wide marginSecond largest, roughly half USDT's scale
Reserve disclosurePublishes periodic reserve reports; reserves mostly short-term US TreasuriesRegular attestations/audits; reserves in cash and short-term Treasuries; stronger transparency reputation
Regulatory postureOffshore-leaning; treatment varies by jurisdictionUS compliance-first; actively licensed and regulator-facing
Where it's usedEverywhere: exchanges, chains, P2P — the deepest liquidityStronger share on regulated venues, DeFi and institutional flows
Shared riskBoth are centralized, company-issued tokens: the issuer can freeze addresses, and reserve trouble can break the peg for either

One-line summary: for pure usability, USDT; for transparency and compliance, USDC. In calm markets the distinction is barely noticeable. It shows up in stress: when an issuer hits the headlines, its token gets sold first and trades at a discount.

Other stablecoins, in one line each

  • DAI: a decentralized stablecoin backed by over-collateralized crypto assets — no single issuer, more complex mechanics. Good to know it exists.
  • USDe and other yield-bearing designs: peg maintained via hedging strategies while paying yield. More complex, more risk — never read the word "stable" as a guarantee.
  • Exchange-house stablecoins: liquidity mostly inside their home ecosystem. Check support before moving them elsewhere.

Depegging: the one big risk

A stablecoin trading away from $1 is called a depeg, and history has real examples: the algorithmic UST collapsed to zero in 2022 and dragged the market down with it; even USDC briefly traded under $0.90 in 2023 when a bank holding part of its reserves failed, before recovering. Two lessons: algorithmic and opaque designs carry the highest risk, and no single stablecoin deserves your entire balance. Large holders split across USDT and USDC and keep an eye on issuer news.

How a beginner should choose

  • Day-to-day trading and P2P deposits: USDT, for the liquidity and merchant depth.
  • Holding a larger amount long-term: USDC, or split between both — while accepting that a stablecoin is not a deposit and has no deposit insurance.
  • "Stablecoin yield" at absurd rates: ask where the yield comes from before anything else. If nobody can answer plainly, walk away — see Common Crypto Scams.

FAQ

Can a stablecoin actually go down?

Yes. Small wobbles (0.99–1.01) are routine, and extreme events have produced major depegs. The peg is a company's promise of full redemption — not a law of physics.

Is USDT issued by a government?

No. USDT is issued by Tether and USDC by Circle — both private companies, which is precisely where issuer risk comes from.

Do I have to buy a stablecoin before buying bitcoin?

Not strictly, but it's the recommended route: most pairs are quoted in USDT, and going through it gets you better prices and depth. Full flow in How to Buy Bitcoin.

Is parking money in stablecoins for yield a good idea?

Exchange earn yields come from lending and platform subsidies, and you carry both platform and issuer counterparty risk. Rates far above market are a prompt to run the scam checklist first and discuss returns second.
