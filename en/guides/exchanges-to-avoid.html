Crypto Exchanges to Avoid (and Why)

Some exchanges are gone because they collapsed. Others are fine companies that simply do not serve your country. Both kinds will waste your time or trap your money if you pick blindly — here is how to tell them apart, using only the public record.

Collapsed exchanges: what the public record shows

Every exchange below is there because of events that are already public record — bankruptcies, court proceedings, and hacks that were reported at the time. None of this is rumour. The point of listing them is not to frighten you away from crypto; it is to show you the exact failure patterns, so you can recognise the warning signs if they ever appear somewhere else.

FTX — collapsed November 2022

FTX was one of the largest crypto exchanges in the world until November 2022, when it collapsed within days after reports that customer funds had been used to support an affiliated trading firm. It filed for bankruptcy, its founder was later convicted of fraud in the United States, and customers became creditors in a bankruptcy process that has stretched on for years. The lesson: size and celebrity endorsements are not evidence of sound custody. If a platform cannot clearly show that customer funds are kept separate from its own trading, that is a reason to leave, not a detail to ignore.

Celsius — collapsed 2022

Celsius offered high yields on crypto deposits, froze withdrawals in June 2022, and filed for bankruptcy the following month. Its business depended on lending customer deposits into risky strategies, and when markets fell, the gap between what it owed depositors and what it held became impossible to close. The lesson: a yield that looks far above the market rate is not free money — it is compensation for risk, and the risk is that the platform cannot return your deposit. Read what a yield product actually does with your funds before you deposit.

Voyager — collapsed 2022

Voyager was a crypto brokerage that filed for bankruptcy in July 2022 after a major borrower failed to repay a large loan. Customers who believed they were holding assets on a straightforward brokerage discovered that their funds had been lent onward, and that recovery depended on a bankruptcy court rather than on their account balance. The lesson: understand the difference between assets held for you and assets lent out on your behalf. If a platform lends customer assets, your risk is the borrower's risk too.

BlockFi — collapsed 2022

BlockFi was a crypto lender that filed for bankruptcy in November 2022, weeks after the FTX collapse, to which it had significant exposure. It had offered interest-bearing accounts that many users treated as savings accounts, but those accounts were unsecured loans to the company, and customers again became bankruptcy creditors. The lesson: a crypto interest account is not a bank account. Bank deposits in many countries carry deposit insurance; crypto lending products generally do not, and "interest account" marketing should never be read as "insured savings".

Mt. Gox — hacked and collapsed 2014

Mt. Gox was once the dominant Bitcoin exchange, handling most of the world's Bitcoin trading, until it collapsed in 2014 after losing a large portion of its customers' Bitcoin to theft over a period of years. The loss was discovered late, the company filed for bankruptcy, and the rehabilitation process for creditors has continued for a decade. The lesson: security practices and transparency matter more than market share. A platform that dominates trading can still have weak internal controls, and early crypto history shows that problems can stay hidden for a long time before they surface.

QuadrigaCX — collapsed 2019

QuadrigaCX was a Canadian exchange that collapsed in 2019 after its founder died, reportedly as the only person who held the passwords to the wallets containing customer funds. Investigators later found that much of the customer funds could not be located, and that the platform had been operating with serious shortfalls. The lesson: a platform with a single point of failure — one person holding the keys, one wallet holding the funds, one auditor nobody can name — is fragile by design. Ask who else controls the money, and what happens if that person disappears.

What these collapses have in common

Different decades, different business models, but the same three patterns repeat. First, customer funds were not truly separate from the company's own money or its trading bets. When a platform can reach into customer deposits to cover its own losses, every customer becomes an unsecured lender without realising it. Second, opacity hid the problem until it was too late. Balance sheets were unpublished, audits were absent or shallow, and users had no way to verify what they were told. Third, growth outran controls: platforms expanded into lending, proprietary trading, or new products faster than their risk management, custody, and compliance could keep up.

That is why the three defences beginners hear about actually matter, and why they matter together rather than alone. Proof of reserves is a platform showing, through a third-party attestation, that it holds the assets it claims to hold. It is useful but limited: it captures one moment in time, it does not by itself prove liabilities, and it is not a full audit — read it as one signal, not a guarantee. Regulation means a platform operates under rules, reporting, and supervision in the jurisdictions where it is licensed, which reduces some risks but removes none of them entirely; regulated firms can still fail. Self-custody means holding your own assets in a wallet where you control the seed phrase, so that no platform's failure can trap them. None of these is sufficient on its own. Together, they are the practical toolkit: prefer platforms that are transparent and supervised, and still do not leave long-term holdings sitting on any platform.

Currently restricted: exchanges that do not serve your market

A second kind of "avoid" has nothing to do with collapse. Some operating exchanges simply do not onboard users in certain countries, for legal and licensing reasons. Signing up with one of these from a restricted country wastes your time: verification will fail, or the account will be limited later. Worse, some users work around restrictions with false location details, which can lead to frozen accounts and lost access — never do this. Red flags to check before trusting any exchange

Use this checklist on any platform, new or established, before you deposit more than a test amount. Anonymous or unverifiable leadership: if you cannot identify who runs the platform, you cannot assess their track record. No licensing or company information: a legitimate platform states where it is incorporated and which regulators supervise it; vagueness here is a choice, not an oversight. Promises of guaranteed returns: no honest platform can guarantee investment returns, and the promise itself is a classic fraud signal. Withdrawal freezes or unexplained delays: occasional maintenance is normal, but repeated or unexplained withdrawal problems are the single most reliable early warning of trouble. Opaque reserves: if a platform publishes no reserves information and no credible attestation, you are trusting its word alone. Pressure to deposit quickly: countdown timers, "limited allocation" claims, and support agents urging larger deposits are sales tactics, not security features.

One more habit belongs on this list: search for the platform's name alongside words like "withdrawal problems", "regulator warning", and "bankruptcy" before you deposit. Ten minutes of searching surfaces problems that marketing pages never mention, and it costs nothing. If the results are dominated by regulator warnings or unresolved withdrawal complaints, that is your answer, regardless of how polished the website looks.

What to do instead

The practical routine is short. First, check the country availability table to find platforms that actually onboard users where you live. Second, prefer platforms that publish reserves information and state their licensing clearly, and treat both as signals rather than guarantees. Third, start with a small test deposit and a small test withdrawal before committing larger amounts — a platform that handles a small withdrawal promptly has passed the only test that matters. Fourth, once you hold an amount that would hurt to lose, learn self-custody: move longer-term holdings to a wallet you control, keep the seed phrase offline and private, and keep only active trading funds on an exchange. Finally, revisit your choices periodically, because availability, licensing, and reserves disclosures all change over time.

The collapses on this page share a final lesson that is easy to miss: in every case, the warning signs existed before the collapse, but they were visible only to people who looked. The users who lost the least were usually the ones who kept records, kept their holdings spread out, and kept most of their crypto somewhere no platform could freeze. That is not paranoia. It is the cheapest insurance in crypto, and it costs nothing but a little attention.

Is FTX still operating?

No. FTX collapsed in November 2022 and went through bankruptcy proceedings. Its trading platform is not operating as a normal exchange, and customers became creditors in the bankruptcy process. If you see a site claiming to be a relaunched FTX, treat it with extreme caution and verify it through official bankruptcy communications before trusting it.

Can I get money back from a collapsed exchange?

Sometimes, but never assume so. Bankruptcy proceedings can return a portion of customer funds over a period of years, and the amount depends on what assets remain and how claims are ranked. Some cases have returned significant amounts, others very little. Treat any money on a collapsed platform as at risk until a court-approved distribution actually reaches you.

Why do people talk about proof of reserves?

Proof of reserves is a way for an exchange to show it holds the assets it claims to hold, usually through a third-party attestation. It matters because several collapsed platforms turned out not to hold what they claimed. But it has limits: it shows assets at one moment in time, not liabilities, and it is not an audit. Read it as one signal among several, not as a guarantee.

Does regulation mean an exchange is safe?

Regulation reduces some risks but removes none of them entirely. A licensed exchange is subject to rules, reporting, and supervision, which is better than no oversight at all. But regulated firms can still fail, freeze withdrawals, or suffer hacks. Regulation is one factor to weigh alongside reserves transparency, track record, and how much you keep on the platform.

Why can I not sign up for an exchange in my country?

Exchanges restrict countries for legal and licensing reasons. For example, our country availability table, checked 30 October 2026, shows Binance not onboarding new users in the EEA and the UK, Bybit not serving the US and Canada, and Bitget restricted in the US, the UK, and Canada with EEA access pending. Availability changes, so check the table and the official page before you plan around any exchange.

Should I keep all my crypto on an exchange?

For most beginners, the practical answer is no, not once the amount matters to you. Keep on an exchange only what you actively trade, and move longer-term holdings to a wallet you control, where you hold the seed phrase. That way, even if a platform fails, your long-term holdings are not trapped in its bankruptcy. Learn self-custody before the amount grows, not after.
