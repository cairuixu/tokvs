Scam Protection: The Fraud Is in People, Not the Tech

The question in the title, answered directly: bitcoin and blockchain technology aren't scams. The scams are run by people borrowing crypto's name. One master rule handles most of them — anyone promising guaranteed, safe, high returns is a scammer. Treat it as absolute and you dodge nine traps out of ten.

8 common crypto scams

1. Fake exchanges and fake apps

Pixel-perfect copies, with the download link sent by a "friend" or group member. Deposits go in, prices move — withdrawals never work. Spot it: download only from official websites and app stores, spell-check the domain character by character. Every entry point on our comparison page leads to an official domain.

2. Signal gurus and paid groups

A "mentor" flaunting screenshot profits leads you into futures or micro-cap calls. His real income is rebates on your fees and losses, plus your subscription. Spot it: a stranger eagerly teaching you a "sure way to make money" is a salesperson, not a teacher.

3. Pig-butchering (romance-investment)

Weeks of relationship-building first, then a casual introduction to an investment platform that only goes up — until the deposit gets big and both money and person vanish. Spot it: never invest through a channel an online acquaintance introduced, especially one that only exists inside some small app.

4. Fake airdrops asking for your seed phrase

Impersonated project pages announce a "claim," and claiming requires entering your seed phrase or private key. The wallet is emptied within minutes. Spot it: anything requesting a private key or seed phrase is a scam, without exception. More in What Is a Crypto Airdrop?

5. Impersonated support

A DM from "support" claims your account is flagged and needs verification — on their page, with a transfer to "unlock" it. Spot it: real support never DMs you first and never asks for transfers. Everything happens inside the app's ticket system.

6. Ponzi "yield" schemes

Dressed up as "AI arbitrage" or "quant bots," promising double-digit monthly returns. Early small withdrawals work — they're paid from new deposits — until the big exit. Spot it: the higher and steadier the promised return, the faster the collapse. If the source of yield can't be explained simply, exclude it.

7. Fake payment proofs in P2P trades

The counterparty sends a doctored payment screenshot and pressures you to release the coins. The money never arrived. Spot it: release only against funds actually settled in your account — screenshots and texts are forgeable. Safe USDT flow: How to Buy USDT.

8. Phishing links and malicious approvals

A link one letter off from the real site; you connect your wallet and sign an approval that lets the attacker drain tokens later. Spot it: enter sites only via your own bookmarks, connect unfamiliar sites with a burner wallet first, and periodically review and revoke approvals you don't recognize. Habits in Wallet & Seed Phrase Safety.

The safety checklist (follow it and you beat 90% of users): top-tier exchanges and official domains only; enable 2FA and an anti-phishing code the day you register; seed phrase on paper only, never in any electronic device; test small before large transfers; never click links strangers send; the moment "guaranteed profit" appears, end the conversation.

If it's already happened

  1. Stop immediately. Do not send any "unlock fee" or "security deposit" — that's the second harvest.
  2. Preserve evidence: chats, transfer records, the counterparty's addresses, URLs, the app installer — screenshot and save everything.
  3. Report to the police at once, with the evidence, and file reports on your national fraud-reporting channels. On-chain transfers are extremely hard to reverse — speed is the only lever you have.
  4. Notify any exchange involved: if funds are still moving through an exchange account, contact its risk team with your police report reference and request a freeze.
  5. Expect the sequel scam: "recovery hackers" and "loss-recovery firms" who find you afterwards are usually the same network's second blade. Anyone charging upfront is lying.

FAQ

Is cryptocurrency itself illegal or a scam?

Technology and fraud are different things. Regulation of crypto trading and mining differs by country and changes over time — check and follow your local rules. This page is risk education, not legal advice.

Someone offered to trade for me and "cover any losses." Believable?

No. The guarantee is a sales line; when losses come, the person disappears or asks for more deposits to "win it back." People who can actually print money don't spend their days recruiting strangers.

Can scammed money be recovered?

Honestly: it's very hard, especially once funds have moved to on-chain addresses. Report, preserve evidence, and try for a freeze — outcomes depend on the case. The only reliable "recovery" is never sending that first transfer.

How do I verify an exchange is real?

Three steps: confirm the official domain from independent public sources; install only from official app stores; deposit small and complete a small withdrawal before scaling up. The full checklist is in How to Choose an Exchange.
