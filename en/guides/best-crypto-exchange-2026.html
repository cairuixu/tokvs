Binance: the default choice

Binance is the largest exchange by volume, and size buys beginners two things: the deepest order books (your market orders fill closest to the quoted price) and the widest payment-rail coverage — card, Apple Pay, bank transfer, SEPA and the largest P2P market, with availability varying by country. The app's "buy crypto" flow is the shortest path from fiat to coins we've tested. Downsides for beginners: the full interface is dense (use the simplified buy flow, not the pro trader), and availability is restricted in some jurisdictions — check your country's status on the official site first. Our Binance safety breakdown covers the trust side in detail.

OKX: the clean all-rounder

OKX's strength is coherence: spot, P2P, earn products and a capable app in one consistent interface, with genuinely good multilingual support. Spot maker fees run slightly lower than Binance's (0.08% vs 0.10% — see our 2026 fee comparison), which matters if you develop a limit-order habit early. For a beginner who wants one account that grows with them — from first buy to more advanced products — OKX is the tidiest package of the three.

Bybit: for the futures-curious

Bybit built its name on derivatives, and it shows: the cleanest futures interface of the three and a product culture oriented toward active traders. As a first exchange it's fully capable — spot buying, P2P and card rails all work — but its edge only matters once you start exploring beyond spot. Our advice: start on spot wherever you open, and let Bybit be your second account if futures interest you later. (And when that day comes, read our futures fee comparison first.)

KYC: what verification actually looks like

All three follow the same regulatory playbook: government ID plus a selfie or short video, matched against your legal name. Typical approval runs minutes to a few hours; delays almost always come from blurry photos or name mismatches. Tips that prevent 90% of rejections: use your ID's exact legal name (no nicknames), photograph the ID in good light with all corners visible, and complete the liveness check in a well-lit room. Nobody enjoys this step; everybody does it once.

Buying your first crypto the easy way

The fastest path on all three: card purchase — debit card or Apple Pay, coins in your account within minutes in most supported regions. Expect a small premium over spot price (the convenience fee is baked into the quote). The cheapest path: P2P — buy USDT from another user via bank transfer, then trade it for whatever you want. It takes longer and demands more attention (see our P2P walkthrough), but the price is usually closest to spot. Start with a small test amount either way — $20–50 teaches the whole flow.

Mistakes beginners make choosing an exchange

  • Chasing the lowest fee. At beginner volumes, fee differences are cents. Interface clarity and support quality matter 10x more.
  • Skipping verification "for later." Unverified accounts hit withdrawal limits fast. Verify on day one.
  • Using a VPN to bypass restrictions. Terms-of-service violation that can freeze withdrawals. Use an exchange that fully serves your country.
  • Keeping everything on the exchange forever. Fine for trading amounts; learn self-custody (see our wallet guide) as your holdings grow.

What about Coinbase and Kraken?

US readers will ask — fairly — why this list has no American exchanges. The honest answer: Coinbase and Kraken are excellent, especially for US users who want domestic regulatory clarity and simple dollar on-ramps. They didn't make this particular top three because, for the beginner criteria we judge on, the three above offer a better combination of global payment-rail coverage, P2P depth and fee levels. If you're in the US, Coinbase's simplicity is genuinely hard to beat for a first purchase — but you'll likely pay more per trade than on the exchanges above, and some advanced products are restricted by region anyway. There's no rule against starting on Coinbase for your first $100 and graduating to a lower-fee venue later. The "best" exchange is allowed to change as you do.

Which crypto exchange is best for complete beginners?

For most first-timers: Binance for the deepest liquidity and simplest buy flow, OKX for a clean all-round product, Bybit if you plan to explore futures early. All three verify identity the same way — the practical differences are interface and available payment rails in your country.

Is it safe to leave money on an exchange?

For trading amounts, yes — the major exchanges invest heavily in security (2FA, cold storage, proof of reserves). For large long-term holdings, learn self-custody with a hardware wallet. Most people do both: trading stack on the exchange, savings in self-custody.

Can I use more than one exchange?

Yes, and many traders do. Multiple accounts let you compare fees, access exclusive listings, and keep a backup during maintenance downtime. There's no cost to holding accounts on all three.

Do I have to pay to open an exchange account?

No. Registration and identity verification are free on all three exchanges. You only pay when you trade (trading fees) or move crypto out (network fees). Anyone asking for an "account opening fee" is running a scam.

Which exchange has the easiest verification?

They're nearly identical: government ID plus a selfie or short video, usually approved within minutes to a few hours. Delays happen with blurry photos or mismatched documents — use good lighting and your legal name exactly as on the ID.

What if my country restricts an exchange?

Check the exchange's official site for your country's status before signing up — full, restricted, or unavailable. Don't use VPNs to bypass restrictions; that's a terms-of-service violation that can freeze withdrawals. Pick the exchange that fully serves where you live.

Should a beginner touch futures in the first month?

No. Learn spot buying, transfers and basic security first — a month of that teaches more than any futures tutorial. When you're ready, our leverage guide explains the risks before you touch the multiplier.
