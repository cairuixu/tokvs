MiCA in plain language

MiCA is short for the Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation, the EU's crypto rulebook. Two parts of it matter for a beginner. First, exchanges — legally called crypto-asset service providers — need authorisation from a regulator in the EEA. A licence granted in one EEA state can be "passported" across all 30 EEA states: the 27 EU members plus Iceland, Norway and Liechtenstein. That is why you see one entity, such as Bybit EU in Austria or OKX Europe in Malta, serving users across the whole EEA. Second, stablecoins: a fiat-pegged token may only be offered to EEA retail users if its issuer holds the required EU authorisation (an e-money institution licence or an asset-referenced token authorisation). Tether, the issuer of USDT, holds neither in the EU — which is why licensed EEA venues cannot offer USDT for trading even though USDT remains the largest stablecoin worldwide.

What changed on 1 July 2026 was the end of the transition period. Firms that had been operating under older national rules had to hold a MiCA licence or wind down their EEA business. That is the date behind the Binance and Bitget rows in the table above, and it is why product ranges on EU entities are narrower than on global platforms: a product that exists globally may simply not be offered on the EU entity. Always check the EU entity's own product list rather than assuming the global catalogue applies to you.

How do you verify a licence claim? Two checks take about a minute. First, the exchange's own legal or entity page should name the entity serving you and its regulator — if it names neither, treat that as a warning sign. Second, the European Securities and Markets Authority (ESMA) keeps a register of licensed crypto-asset service providers; search the entity name there. A licence claim that cannot be found in either place is not a claim worth trusting with your money.

For an EEA resident, the on-ramp is your bank account or your card — not the P2P markets that dominate guides written for other regions. P2P on a global platform is a separate product from the EU entity that serves you, so use the deposit options inside the EU entity's own app or site.

Step by step: your first buy

The steps below assume Bybit EU, the licensed EEA route in the table above. If you choose OKX Europe instead, follow the same steps but buy USDC, not USDT — OKX Europe does not offer USDT trading. Default to USDC on either venue: USDT spot pairs have been delisted for EEA retail on MiCA-licensed venues, so treat USDT as the exception you must confirm, not the plan.

  1. Go to bybit.eu, not the global site. EEA residents are served by the EU entity. An account on the global platform is not the same thing, and it is not the account you want.
  2. Sign up with email and a strong, unique password. Use an email address you control and a password you do not reuse anywhere else.
  3. Complete identity verification (KYC). You will need a government-issued photo ID and, usually, proof of address. Approval typically takes minutes to a few hours; blurry document photos cause most rejections, so retake them in good light if it stalls.
  4. Turn on security before depositing. Enable two-factor authentication with an authenticator app (not SMS) and set the anti-phishing code if the platform offers one. Do this before any money moves.
  5. Check whether USDT is listed for your country — expect it not to be. Search for USDT on bybit.eu. MiCA-licensed venues delisted USDT pairs for EEA retail during 2026, so the expected case is that USDT is not offered for trading. If it is not listed, buy USDC instead using the same steps. Do not deposit hoping it appears.
  6. Deposit euros via SEPA. Copy the exchange's deposit details exactly from its own deposit page — never from a chat message or a screenshot someone sent you. Send a small test amount first, €20–50, and wait for it to arrive.
  7. Place the buy. Go to Spot, open the USDC pair shown for your deposit currency (or the USDT pair only if step 5 confirmed it is listed for your country), and place a market order. Read the preview screen carefully: it states the amount, the fee and exactly how much you will receive. Only then confirm.
  8. Verify and log it. Check the balance in your account, then write down the date, euros paid, coins received and the order ID. That record is your cost basis if you ever sell, and it takes ten seconds now versus hours of reconstruction later.

Once the test buy feels boring, you have learned the flow. Repeat at your real size — boring is the goal, because boring means no surprises.

USDT networks: TRC20 vs ERC20

USDT exists on several blockchains, and the network matters every time you deposit or withdraw. The two you will meet most often are TRC20 and ERC20. TRC20 means USDT on the Tron network: transfers are usually fast, and the fee is often around 1 USDT — always check the fee shown on the withdrawal preview, because it can change. ERC20 means USDT on the Ethereum network: it is supported almost everywhere, but the gas fee varies with network congestion and can be much higher than TRC20.

The rule that prevents losses is simple: the network must match on both ends. Sending TRC20 USDT to an ERC20 address, or ERC20 USDT to a TRC20 address, can lose the funds. Before any transfer, open the deposit page of the receiving platform and check which networks it accepts for USDT; then select that same network on the sending side. Other networks exist too — Solana, BNB Chain and others — but only use one if both sides support it. And as with deposits, send a small test amount first when you move USDT to a new address.

If you already hold funds on an exchange that stopped serving you

Withdrawals-only is not the same as funds gone, so do not panic — but do not drift either. Work through this in order:

  1. Read the platform's official notice. Withdrawal deadlines and wind-down windows are stated there, not in social media threads. If a deadline exists, it is the one fact you need first.
  2. Pick a destination before you move anything. Either a MiCA-licensed platform that serves your country, or a wallet you control (self-custody). Moving without a destination is how funds end up parked on a second platform that also does not serve you.
  3. Withdraw a small test amount first. Match the network on both ends — TRC20 to a TRC20 address, never to an ERC20 address — and confirm the test arrives before sending the rest.
  4. Do not deposit new funds into an account that is winding down. Deposits into a withdrawals-only account create a second problem on top of the first one.
  5. Ignore migration-season scammers. Wind-downs attract fake support accounts offering to "help" move your funds. Real support never asks for your password, your two-factor codes or your seed phrase, and no legitimate helper needs remote access to your account.
  6. If your funds are USDT, check the platform's official migration path. Some licensed venues offer a one-way conversion from USDT into USDC for EEA users; terms and availability change, so confirm the current option on the official page before sending anything, and act early rather than near a deadline.

USDC: the MiCA-authorised alternative

USDC is a dollar-pegged stablecoin issued by Circle, and unlike USDT it holds EU authorisation — which is why it is fully supported on licensed EEA venues for deposit, trading and withdrawal. For most beginner purposes it does exactly the job USDT does: a dollar-pegged coin for buying BTC or ETH, or for parking value between trades. The price behaviour is the same idea — one coin tracking one US dollar — and the trading pairs on licensed EEA platforms are quoted against USDC rather than USDT.

OKX Europe is the clearest example: because it cannot offer USDT trading, it points EEA users to USDC (and USDG) instead. If you hold USDT elsewhere and want to bring the value into a licensed EEA venue, check that venue's official page for its conversion option before moving anything — some venues offer a one-way USDT-to-USDC conversion, and the terms differ by platform. Do not assume a conversion exists; verify it, then move a test amount first like any other transfer.

What it costs

Do not trust a single advertised fee, because the cost of a buy stacks up from four places: the deposit fee (SEPA usually costs less than card), the trading fee (check the EU entity's own official fee schedule — EU entities can price differently from their global platforms, so a global fee table may not apply to you), the spread (the small gap between the buy and sell price), and the withdrawal or network fee (shown on the withdrawal preview). The preview screen before you confirm is the real price: it states exactly how much USDT or USDC you will receive. If that number surprises you, stop and compare routes before confirming — the preview is there to be read, not clicked through.

Keeping your coins safe

Three options, same as everywhere: on the exchange (convenient for trading, carries platform risk), in your own wallet (you hold the seed phrase — the sensible move once the amount matters to you), or in yield products (extra return, extra risk — read the terms before touching them). For a first purchase, leaving it on the exchange while you learn is perfectly fine. Once the amount grows, learn self-custody: our crypto wallets for beginners guide covers seed phrases properly. Whatever you choose, the non-negotiables are two-factor authentication with an authenticator app, a unique password, and a seed phrase stored offline that you never photograph, never type into a website and never share with anyone.

Mistakes EEA beginners make

  • Signing up on the global site instead of the EU entity. The account, the product list and the protections are not the same. Start on the EU entity.
  • Depositing USDT to OKX Europe expecting to trade it. USDT cannot be traded there. Check the pair list before you send anything.
  • Sending USDT on the wrong network. TRC20 to an ERC20 address, or the reverse, can lose the funds. Match the network on both ends, test small first.
  • Using someone else's bank account for SEPA. Name mismatches get deposits rejected. Your account, your name, always.
  • Skipping the test amount. A €20 test buy that reveals a problem is cheap; a full-size transfer that reveals the same problem is not.
  • Trusting "support" messages during a migration. Wind-down periods are prime scam season. Support never asks for passwords or seed phrases.
  • Assuming global fees apply to the EU entity. EU entities set their own fee schedules. Read the EU entity's official fee page.

The full first-buy checklist (EEA edition)

  1. Confirm the exchange serving you is MiCA-licensed — entity name and regulator on its legal page, and the entity searchable in the ESMA register.
  2. Sign up on the EU entity (bybit.eu for Bybit), complete KYC, enable two-factor authentication and the anti-phishing code.
  3. Check USDT is listed for your country on the EU entity. If it is not, switch the plan to USDC before depositing anything.
  4. Deposit a €20–50 test amount via SEPA from a bank account in your own name.
  5. Place a small market buy on Spot. Read the preview — amount, fee, coins received — then confirm.
  6. Verify the balance, log the trade (date, euros, coins, order ID), and only then repeat at your real size.
  7. If you hold funds on a platform that stopped serving the EEA, work the stranded-funds steps above early, and never deposit more into it.

Can I still buy USDT if I live in the EEA?

It depends on the platform. MiCA-licensed EEA platforms must follow the stablecoin rules, and some have removed USDT trading pairs entirely, so always check the live pair list on the platform's EU site before you deposit. Bybit EU serves EEA residents under its Austrian MiCA authorisation, and OKX Europe serves the EEA but does not offer USDT trading — its dollar stablecoin option is USDC. If USDT is not listed for you, USDC works the same way for most purposes, such as buying BTC or ETH.

Why can't I trade USDT on OKX Europe?

Because Tether, the issuer of USDT, has no MiCA authorisation in the EU, and OKX Europe is a MiCA-licensed exchange. Under MiCA, a licensed exchange may only offer stablecoins whose issuer holds the required EU authorisation. That is also why OKX Europe points EEA users to USDC, issued by Circle, which does hold EU authorisation. Do not deposit USDT to OKX Europe expecting to trade it — check the official OKX Europe page first.

Is Binance still available in the EEA?

Binance no longer takes new activity from EEA residents: it withdrew its Greek MiCA application on 24 June 2026 and has offered withdrawals only since 1 July 2026. If you already hold funds there, withdraw them to a licensed platform or to a wallet you control, and check the official Binance notice for any deadline. Do not deposit new funds expecting to trade.

What is MiCA, in plain language?

MiCA is the EU's crypto rulebook. Since 1 July 2026, only exchanges licensed under MiCA may onboard EEA residents, and a licence from one EEA regulator can be passported across all 30 EEA states — the 27 EU members plus Iceland, Norway and Liechtenstein. For a beginner it means two things: check that the exchange serving you is MiCA-licensed (the ESMA register lists licensed firms), and expect stablecoin choices to be narrower, because only authorised stablecoins may be offered.

Should I buy USDT or USDC in the EEA?

If you live in the EEA, USDC is the simpler choice on licensed platforms: it is MiCA-authorised, fully supported for deposit, trading and withdrawal on OKX Europe and other licensed venues, and it serves the same purpose as USDT — a dollar-pegged coin for buying other crypto. USDT still exists and can be held in your own wallet, but licensed EEA exchanges may not offer it for trading, so check the pair list before you plan around it.

I already hold crypto on an exchange that stopped serving EEA residents — what should I do?

Do not panic, and do not deposit more. First, read the platform's official notice for its withdrawal deadline. Then withdraw to a MiCA-licensed platform or to a wallet you control, sending a small test amount first and matching the network on both ends — for example, TRC20 to a TRC20 address, never to an ERC20 address. Keep records of every withdrawal, and ignore anyone who contacts you claiming to be support during a migration: real support never asks for your password or seed phrase.
