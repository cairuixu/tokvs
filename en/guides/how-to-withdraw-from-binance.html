First: what "withdraw" means on Binance

Beginners often trip on one distinction, so let us clear it up first. On Binance, "withdraw" covers two different things. A fiat withdrawal sends regular money (dollars, euros, or your local currency) to your bank account. A crypto withdrawal sends coins or tokens to a wallet address on a blockchain. Which one you need depends on what you want at the end: spendable money in your bank, or crypto under your own control. There is also a third route that blurs the line — selling on P2P, where a buyer pays you directly by bank transfer — and for many countries it is the most practical way to cash out. This guide covers all three, then the mistakes that cause most stuck withdrawals.

Before any withdrawal, one housekeeping note: your funds live in different wallets inside Binance (Spot, Funding, Earn, Futures), and you can only withdraw from the right one. If the withdraw button looks greyed out or your balance shows zero, you probably need to transfer funds internally first — for example, from Funding to Spot. Internal transfers are free and instant, and they confuse almost every beginner exactly once.

Route 1: withdraw fiat to your bank account

This is the cleanest route when it is available in your country. The steps: sell your crypto for your local currency on the spot market (for example, sell USDT for USD or EUR), then go to Wallet, choose Withdraw, select Fiat, pick your currency and your linked bank account, enter the amount, and confirm. Your bank account must be in the same name as your verified Binance account, or the withdrawal will be rejected or frozen for review.

Timing depends entirely on your country's payment rails. Instant bank systems can settle in minutes; traditional transfers can take one to several business days, and weekends and holidays add delays. Fees also vary by currency and rail — Binance shows the exact fee on the withdrawal screen before you confirm, and that screen is the authority: fee schedules change, so never rely on a remembered number. If fiat withdrawal is not offered for your currency at all, skip to Route 2 — that is exactly the situation P2P exists for.

Route 2: sell on P2P and get paid by bank transfer

P2P is the cash-out route for most of the world, and it works like the buy flow in reverse. You go to P2P, choose Sell, pick the coin (usually USDT) and your currency, and browse buyers. Pick a buyer with a long history, hundreds of completed trades, and a high completion rate — the same checklist as buying, because the same scammers work both sides. Enter the amount, place the order, and your crypto locks in exchange escrow.

Then the buyer sends you a bank transfer (or mobile-money payment) for the agreed amount. When the money lands in your account — verified in your own banking app, not from a screenshot the buyer sends you — you release the crypto from escrow. That order matters: never release before the money is actually in your account. Two safety rules complete the picture. First, the payment must come from an account in the buyer's own name, matching their P2P identity; third-party payments are a classic fraud pattern and a reason to appeal. Second, keep all communication inside the P2P chat — if a buyer asks to move to WhatsApp or Telegram, that is a red flag, because off-platform chat removes your evidence if you need to appeal.

P2P pricing deserves one honest note: buyers quote slightly below the market rate, and that gap is their margin. On liquid currencies the gap is small; on less common ones it widens. Compare three or four buyers before accepting, and treat an offer far above market as bait, not luck. Our how to sell crypto guide covers the selling mindset in more detail.

Route 3: withdraw crypto to your own wallet

This route is for moving crypto into self-custody, not for cashing out — and it is where the expensive mistakes happen. The steps: in your wallet app, copy the receiving address for the coin; on Binance, go to Withdraw, select Crypto, choose the coin, paste the address, select the network, enter the amount, and confirm with 2FA. Then comes the part beginners rush: the network.

The network choice: the most expensive dropdown on Binance

Many coins exist on several blockchains at once. USDT, for example, runs on Ethereum (ERC20), Tron (TRC20), BNB Chain (BEP20), and others. These are not interchangeable. If your wallet shows you a Tron address and you withdraw USDT on Ethereum, the funds go to an address your wallet may not control on that chain — and recovery ranges from painful to impossible.

The rule is absolute: the network you select on Binance must match the network your receiving wallet or exchange supports for that coin. Check both sides before confirming. If your wallet supports several networks, pick the one with the lowest fee shown on Binance's withdrawal screen — fees differ sharply by network, and the withdrawal screen shows the exact fee per network before you confirm. For a first withdrawal to a new address, send a tiny test amount, confirm it arrives, and only then send the rest. The test costs one extra fee; skipping it has cost beginners their entire withdrawal.

Two address traps: 1. never type an address by hand — copy and paste it, then check the first and last four characters match; malware exists that swaps clipboard addresses. 2. some coins (XRP, XLM, and a few others) need a memo or destination tag as well as the address when sending to an exchange — if the receiving side shows a memo field, fill it in exactly, or the deposit can be delayed for weeks.

One cost beginners underestimate: withdrawing tiny amounts. Every crypto withdrawal carries a flat network fee regardless of size, so withdrawing 10 dollars of a coin with a 5-dollar network fee is a 50% haircut. If the amount is small, either pick the cheapest network available, accumulate a larger amount before withdrawing, or use P2P instead. Fees and minimums are displayed on the withdrawal page before you confirm — treat that page as the final word, since networks and fee schedules change.

Why withdrawals get stuck (and what to do)

  • Security lock after account changes. Changing your password, resetting 2FA, or adding a new withdrawal address typically triggers a 24-hour withdrawal hold. This is protection, not a bug — wait it out, and do not try to "fix" it by changing more settings, which can restart the clock.
  • Network congestion or wallet maintenance. If the blockchain is congested or Binance has paused withdrawals for that coin's maintenance, your withdrawal queues. Check the withdrawal status page and any maintenance notice before panicking.
  • Wrong network or missing memo. If you sent on an unsupported network or forgot a required memo, contact support immediately with the transaction ID — recovery is sometimes possible but slow, and never guaranteed. This is why the test amount exists.
  • Incomplete verification or limits. Unverified or partially verified accounts have withdrawal limits; a withdrawal above your limit will be blocked until verification completes. Check your verification level in account settings.
  • Pending email confirmation. Some withdrawals require clicking a confirmation link sent to your email. Check your inbox (and spam folder) — and confirm the email carries your anti-phishing code, as covered in our account safety checklist, before clicking.

If a withdrawal is genuinely stuck beyond the expected window, gather three things before contacting support: the withdrawal record (coin, amount, network, time), the transaction ID if one was generated, and the receiving address. Support can only trace what you can show them. And never pay a third party who promises to "unblock" your withdrawal — that is a follow-up scam aimed at people already anxious about stuck funds.

One more route: exchange-to-exchange transfers

Sometimes the destination is not your bank or your wallet but another exchange — for example, moving USDT to a platform that lists a coin Binance does not, or consolidating funds. The mechanics are identical to Route 3: copy the deposit address from the receiving exchange, match the network exactly, mind the memo if the coin requires one, and send a test amount first. The extra trap here is assuming two exchanges support the same networks for a coin; they often do not, and the deposit page of the receiving exchange — not your memory — is the authority on which networks it accepts. Treat an exchange deposit exactly like a wallet withdrawal: same checklist, same test amount, same irreversibility if you get the network wrong. Other major exchanges such as Bybit work the same way, and the same rules protect you there.

Withdrawal safety checklist

  1. Funds moved to the right internal wallet (Spot or Funding as required).
  2. Receiving address copied, not typed; first and last four characters checked.
  3. Network matched on both sides; memo or tag filled in if required.
  4. Test amount sent first for any new address or network.
  5. Fee and minimum checked on the withdrawal screen before confirming.
  6. For P2P: money verified in your own bank app before releasing escrow.
  7. For bank: account name matches your verified Binance name.
  8. Withdrawal whitelist enabled, so a compromised account cannot redirect your funds.

Withdrawals feel stressful the first time because they are irreversible — there is no "undo" button on a blockchain transfer. That is exactly why the checklist exists. Follow it mechanically, and the process becomes boring. Boring is the goal: a withdrawal you barely thought about is a withdrawal done right.

How long does a Binance withdrawal take?

It depends on the route. Crypto withdrawals usually arrive in minutes to an hour once the network confirms them, though congested networks take longer. Bank withdrawals depend on your country's payment rails and can take from minutes to several business days. P2P sales settle as fast as the buyer pays — often within minutes.

Why is my Binance withdrawal stuck on processing?

The usual causes are a security lock after a password or 2FA change, a new withdrawal address still inside its waiting period, network congestion, wallet maintenance on that coin, or an incomplete verification step. Check the withdrawal status page first, then your email for a security notice, before contacting support.

What happens if I pick the wrong network for a withdrawal?

Sending crypto on a network the receiving wallet does not support can mean the funds are lost or very difficult to recover. Always match the network on both sides — if your wallet shows a TRON address, withdraw on TRON, not Ethereum. When in doubt, send a tiny test amount first.

Do I need a memo or tag when withdrawing?

Some coins, such as XRP and XLM, require a memo or destination tag in addition to the address when sending to an exchange. If the receiving side shows a memo field, you must fill it in exactly — withdrawals sent without a required memo can be delayed for weeks or lost. Wallet-to-wallet transfers of those coins usually do not need a memo.

Is there a minimum withdrawal amount on Binance?

Yes, every coin and network has its own minimum withdrawal amount, shown on the withdrawal page before you confirm. If your balance is below the minimum for your chosen network, switch to a network with a lower minimum or a different route such as P2P.

Can I withdraw USDT from Binance to my bank account?

Not directly — banks do not accept USDT. You first convert USDT to your local currency, either by selling it on the spot market and withdrawing fiat, or by selling it to a P2P buyer who pays you by bank transfer. Both routes end with regular money in your bank account.
