Maker vs. taker in 30 seconds

A maker order sits on the book waiting (a limit order that doesn't fill instantly) — you add liquidity, so you pay less. A taker order fills immediately against the book (market orders, or aggressive limits) — you remove liquidity, so you pay more. The single cheapest habit in crypto trading: use limit orders by default. On every exchange above, that one habit cuts your fee by 20–50% versus market-ordering everything.

Where fees actually bite: spot vs. futures

Spot fees look small because they are — 0.10% on a $1,000 buy is $1. Futures are the opposite: leverage multiplies your traded notional, and the fee multiplies with it. A 10x leveraged $1,000 position pays fees on $10,000 of notional. That's why futures traders should care about every 0.005%, while spot buy-and-hold investors can nearly ignore the whole topic.

The hidden fourth fee: withdrawals

Withdrawal fees aren't set by fee tiers — they're set by blockchain networks. Withdrawing USDT on TRC20 usually costs around $1; on ERC20 it can cost $10+ when Ethereum is congested. Same exchange, same coin, 10x difference — determined entirely by the network you pick. Always check the withdrawal fee preview before confirming, and never withdraw dust amounts.

Five ways to actually pay less

  1. Limit orders, always. Maker rates beat taker rates on every platform. (See above.)

  5. Don't "Convert" large amounts. One-tap convert features quote a spread; for size, a spot limit order is measurably cheaper.

Fees at three volumes: worked examples

Casual — $2,000/month spot, mixed maker/taker (~0.09% blended): about $1.80/month everywhere. The "cheapest exchange" debate is worth $0.20/month to you. Skip it; pick by experience.

Active — $50,000/month spot, mostly maker: OKX at 0.08% = $40; others at 0.10% = $50. Ten dollars a month, $120 a year — real but not life-changing.

Serious futures — $500,000/month taker: Binance/OKX at 0.05% = $250; Bybit at 0.055% = $275; Bitget at 0.06% = $300. Now we're talking $300–600/year gaps — before rebates. At this level, the fee table is your most-read page.

The rebate layer most people ignore

Posted fees are the sticker price; rebates are the discount you get for showing up through the right door. Under official referral programs, a slice of every fee you pay comes back — same fee rate as signing up directly, plus cashback on top. At the "serious futures" volume above, a 30–50% rebate returns $900–1,500 a year. That's not a coupon; that's a salary bump. The rebate math deserves its own line in your spreadsheet.

Deposit and withdrawal: the forgotten costs

  • Deposits: crypto deposits are free on all four majors. Fiat deposits vary: SEPA/bank transfer usually free on the exchange side; card deposits carry processing fees.
  • Withdrawals: set by blockchain networks, not fee tiers. USDT-TRC20 ≈ $1; USDT-ERC20 can hit $10+ in congestion. Same coin, 10x gap — the network dropdown matters more than the exchange logo.
  • Internal transfers: moving funds between sub-accounts or between spot/futures wallets inside one exchange is free and instant. Use it instead of on-chain moves.

Myths about "zero fees"

  • "Zero-fee trading weeks." Usually maker-only, or capped, or marketing for a new listing. Read the asterisks.
  • "0% fees forever." If an unknown exchange promises this, ask how it makes money. The answer is usually: your deposits.
  • "Fee tokens always save 25%." Paying fees in BNB/OKB-style tokens does discount fees on some platforms — but the discount shrinks as tiers change, and holding the token is itself a position. Do the math; don't assume.

The psychology of fee-chasing (read before optimizing)

There's a documented beginner trap: spending six hours comparing 0.01% fee differences, then market-buying into a 5% intraday swing. Fees are certain, small, and controllable; market moves are uncertain, large, and uncontrollable. Optimize in the right order: 1. stop market-ordering everything (saves 20–50% of fees instantly), 2. use a referral link (free money), 3. then compare platforms. Anyone doing step 3 before step 1 is polishing the wrong knob.

The pre-trade fee checklist (30 seconds)

  1. Limit or market? (Limit = maker = cheaper.)
  2. Spot or futures? (Futures notional = the real fee base.)
  3. Which network for withdrawal? (Set before you buy, not after.)
  4. Rebate attached? (Signed up via referral = yes.)

Run this checklist for a month and low fees become a habit, not a research project.

When fees genuinely decide the platform

Three profiles where the fee table is the whole decision: high-frequency futures traders (basis points compound into salaries), market makers (negative maker fees exist at volume — the exchange pays you), and arbitrageurs (their edge is the fee gap). If you're none of these — and 95% of readers aren't — treat fees as a tiebreaker between otherwise-equal platforms, not the main event.

Rebate math: the worked example

Take the "serious futures" trader: $500k/month taker volume on Binance at 0.05% = $250/month in fees, $3,000/year. With a 40% rebate through an official referral program, $1,200/year comes back. Same trades, same platform, same fee rate — the only difference is which link you signed up with. That's the entire pitch for referral links: not lower fees, but a refund on the fees you were paying anyway. It costs nothing, it's the exchange's own program, and the only mistake is signing up without one.

Fee structures to avoid entirely

  • Spread-only "no fee" brokers quoting 1–2% spreads — that's a 1–2% fee wearing a costume.
  • Withdrawal fees above network cost by 5x+ — some small platforms monetize exits. Check before depositing, not after.
  • "Premium" tiers you pay for monthly that only make sense above volumes you'll never hit. Do the breakeven math first.

What's the cheapest way to buy $100 of bitcoin?

A limit order on any major's spot market: ~$0.08–0.10 in fees. Don't overthink it — the price move in the next hour dwarfs the fee.

Do VIP tiers matter for regular users?

Not until your 30-day volume crosses into the tens of thousands. Below that you're on the standard tier everywhere, which is exactly what our table shows.

Are decentralized exchanges cheaper?

Sometimes on paper, never all-in: gas fees, MEV/slippage and bridge costs usually exceed CEX fees for regular sizes. DEXes win on custody and access, not on cost.

Should I switch exchanges just for lower fees?

Do the math first: compute your actual yearly fee gap (our calculator), then ask if it's worth re-verifying, re-learning an app, and splitting your liquidity. For most people under $50k/year volume, the answer is no.

Do fees apply when I just hold crypto?

No. Holding costs nothing on any major exchange. Fees trigger on trades, conversions and withdrawals — not on sitting still.

Do fees differ between the app and the website?

No — the same account, same fee tier, same rates on app, web and API. Pick whichever interface you trade best on.
