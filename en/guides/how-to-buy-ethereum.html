How to Buy Ethereum: The Complete Beginner Process

Ethereum is the second-largest crypto and the backbone of DeFi, NFTs, and stablecoins — and buying your first ETH takes about half an hour: pick a top-tier exchange, verify your ID, fund your account, place a spot order, then decide where to keep it. The only part that trips beginners up is gas fees and network choice, and this guide covers both before you spend a cent.

The 5 steps at a glance: 1. Pick an exchange → 2. Sign up and verify → 3. Fund your account → 4. Place your ETH order → 5. Store it safely. Each step is expanded below — follow along, everything works from your phone.

Before you start: three things to prepare

  • A government ID. Every legitimate exchange requires identity verification (KYC), no exceptions. Any channel offering "no-ID buying" is a trap.
  • A phone and an email you control — for sign-up, codes, and an authenticator app.
  • Money you can afford to lose. Start small — enough to learn the process, not enough to hurt.

One line on what you're buying: ETH is the native coin of the Ethereum network. It pays transaction fees (called "gas") and powers the apps built on Ethereum — lending, trading, NFTs, and stablecoin transfers all run on it. That dual role is exactly why ETH stays in demand, and also why gas fees matter to you as a holder.

Step 1: Pick an exchange

An exchange is the marketplace where you buy. Beginners should only choose from the global top tier — large, long-running, with transparent fees. Binance, OKX, and Bybit all list deep ETH spot markets. Their regular-user spot fees are close, so pick on app experience and the fiat rails available in your country. Step 2: Sign up and verify

  1. Download the app only from the official website or your phone's app store. Never install an app from a link someone sent you — fake apps are the number-one beginner scam.
  2. Register with email or phone and a unique, strong password.
  3. Complete identity verification: upload your ID and pass a face check. Approval usually takes minutes to a few hours.
  4. Immediately enable two-factor authentication (2FA) and the anti-phishing code. This is your account's basic armor.

The detailed walkthrough is in Step 3 of the Beginner Path: Sign Up.

Step 3: Fund your account

You have two routes. The standard one: buy USDT first, then trade it for ETH on the ETH/USDT pair — USDT pairs have the deepest liquidity and the tightest prices. The simpler one: some exchanges offer direct fiat pairs (ETH/USD, ETH/EUR) or instant card buy, which skips the USDT step but can cost more. Either way, start with a small test amount and confirm it lands before doing more. The USDT route is covered in How to Buy USDT.

Step 4: Place your ETH order

  1. In spot trading, open the ETH/USDT pair.
  2. Beginners should use a limit order: set the price you're willing to pay and the amount (say, $100 worth). It fills when the market reaches your price, and maker fees are lower. In a hurry? A market order works, at whatever the current book price is.
  3. Check three numbers before confirming: price, amount, fee. At a 0.10% spot fee, a $100 buy costs about $0.10. Fee math is covered in Crypto Fees Explained.
  4. Once filled, your ETH shows under Assets / Spot. Congratulations — first purchase done.

Step 5: Decide where to keep it

  • Small amounts, still trading: leaving it on the exchange is fine — with 2FA on and your account never shared.
  • Larger amounts, long-term holding: move it to a wallet you control, seed phrase backed up on paper. See Step 5 of the path and Wallet & Seed Phrase Safety.

Gas fees and network choice: the part beginners get wrong

Buying ETH on an exchange costs you only the trading fee. The expensive mistakes happen when you move ETH — and they all come down to two concepts: gas and networks.

What gas is. Every transaction on the Ethereum network pays a fee in ETH, called gas. Gas prices rise and fall with network congestion: when everyone is trading at once, fees spike; at quiet hours they drop. You don't set gas when buying on an exchange, but you do pay it indirectly when you withdraw ETH on-chain or use Ethereum apps — and the withdrawal screen always shows the fee before you confirm, so read it instead of tapping through.

Mainnet vs. layer-2 networks. Ethereum's main network (ERC-20) is the default, but cheaper "layer-2" networks like Arbitrum, Optimism, and Base now carry a huge share of activity. The catch: ETH on Arbitrum is not the same balance as ETH on mainnet. They look identical, they use the same address format, but they live on different networks and do not cross over without a bridge. Sending mainnet ETH to a wallet that only watches Arbitrum — or withdrawing via the wrong network option — strands your coins until you bridge them back, and bridging costs time and fees.

The ETH transfer rules: 1. On the withdrawal screen, the network you select must match the network your receiving wallet or exchange deposit actually uses — read both sides, don't guess. 2. Never send ETH to a contract address or a deposit address shown for a different network. 3. First transfer to a new destination is always a small test amount; only move the rest after it arrives.

A practical habit that saves real money: check the withdrawal fee and the network before every transfer, and avoid moving ETH during obvious congestion peaks if the transfer isn't urgent. Gas is a fee you can often shrink just by waiting an hour.

One more distinction worth knowing before your first withdrawal: the fee the exchange shows you is its own withdrawal charge, not the network's live gas price. The two are related but not identical — exchanges typically set a flat or tiered withdrawal fee that already bundles the gas cost, so the number you see is the number you pay. If that number looks high compared to what friends report, check whether you selected mainnet when a layer-2 option was available for your destination, and whether the transfer can wait for a quieter window. Either adjustment can cut the cost noticeably, and neither requires any technical skill — just a habit of reading the withdrawal screen twice.

How much should you buy the first time?

The honest answer: an amount you wouldn't lose sleep over. Start small (say, $50–100) and run the entire flow end to end — deposit, buy, and if you like, withdraw — with zero mistakes. If you're in it for the long term, consider putting a fixed small sum in every month to average your entry price. Your first purchase isn't about profit. It's about completing the process flawlessly.

Five buying mistakes beginners keep making

  1. Buying with borrowed money or credit — leverage on a first purchase turns normal volatility into a crisis.
  2. Paying peak gas without checking — withdrawing during congestion can cost multiples of the quiet-hour fee.
  3. Wrong-network transfers — the most expensive ETH beginner mistake, and entirely avoidable with the test-amount habit.
  4. Keeping everything on one platform — an exchange is not a bank. Move larger long-term holdings to your own wallet.
  5. Following "gurus" and signal groups — anyone telling you what to buy profits from your fees and your exit liquidity.

FAQ

How much ETH can I buy with $50?

ETH divides into tiny fractions, so $50 buys a small piece of one ETH. The per-coin price is irrelevant — what matters is the percentage move. Most beginners start with $50–100 worth.

Do I pay gas fees when buying ETH on an exchange?

No. Buying on an exchange's spot market only costs the trading fee (around 0.1%). Gas fees apply when you move ETH on the Ethereum network or use on-chain apps. Withdrawal fees are separate and are shown before you confirm.

Should I keep ETH on the exchange or move it to a wallet?

Small amounts you still trade can stay on the exchange with 2FA enabled. Larger long-term holdings belong in a wallet you control, with the seed phrase backed up on paper. See Wallet & Seed Phrase Safety for the full routine.

Can I send ETH to any wallet address?

Only to an address on the same network you selected for the transfer. ETH on Ethereum mainnet and ETH on a layer-2 network look identical but do not cross over without a bridge. Always match the network on the withdrawal screen with the network your wallet expects, and test with a small amount first.
