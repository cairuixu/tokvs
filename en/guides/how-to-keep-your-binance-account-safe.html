How to Keep Your Binance Account Safe

Most "hacked exchange account" stories are not exchange hacks at all. They are reused passwords, missing 2FA, and one careless click. This checklist closes all three holes in about ten minutes.

The 30-second truth about Binance safety

Here is the uncomfortable fact that shapes everything below: when beginners lose crypto, the exchange's vault is almost never the problem. The pattern repeats thousands of times a year. Someone reuses a password that leaked in an old data breach. Someone logs in through a Google ad that leads to a lookalike domain. Someone trusts a "support agent" on Telegram who asks for a verification code. The funds leave through the front door, with valid credentials, and no security system on earth can tell that login apart from yours.

That is actually good news. It means the biggest risks are the ones you can eliminate yourself, for free, in one sitting. Binance's platform-side protections — the SAFU emergency fund, proof of reserves, cold storage for the bulk of assets — cover the exchange's side of the bargain. This guide covers yours. Do the whole checklist once, and the most common attack paths against your account simply stop working.

Step 1: a password that exists nowhere else

Your Binance password must be unique to Binance — not a variation of your email password, not your old favorite password with a "!" added. Credential-stuffing attacks work by trying passwords leaked from other sites against crypto exchanges, and they succeed at scale precisely because people reuse passwords. A password manager solves this properly: it generates a long random password, remembers it, and fills it in for you. If you will not use a manager, write a long passphrase on paper and keep it somewhere safe — never in a notes app, a chat message to yourself, or a screenshot.

Two more rules. First, never type your password anywhere except the real Binance app or the real binance.com domain, which you reached by typing it yourself or from a bookmark — never from a link in an email, ad, or message. Second, change the password immediately if you ever suspect it leaked, and treat any unexpected "password reset" email as a warning sign, not an instruction to follow.

Step 2: authenticator 2FA, not SMS

Two-factor authentication is the single highest-value setting on this page, and the type matters. SMS codes feel convenient, but they can be intercepted through SIM-swap attacks: a scammer convinces your mobile carrier to move your number to a SIM they control, and suddenly your texted codes arrive on their phone. It happens to ordinary people, not just celebrities.

An authenticator app (Google Authenticator, Authy, or similar) generates time-based codes on your own device. There is no carrier to trick and no text to intercept. Set it up in the Binance app under Security, and when it shows you the backup key, save that key offline — on paper, in a safe place. If you lose your phone without the backup key, recovery becomes a slow identity-verification process. If you keep the backup key, recovery takes minutes.

Never share a 2FA code with anyone, ever. No Binance employee, no support agent, no "security team" will ever ask for it. Anyone asking is a scammer, full stop — including people who sound professional and already know your name.

Step 3: set your anti-phishing code

This is the most underused safety feature on Binance, and it takes thirty seconds. In your security settings you can set a short word or phrase — pick something only you would recognize. From then on, every genuine email from Binance includes that code. An email claiming to be from Binance that lacks your code is fake, no matter how convincing the logo, layout, or urgent warning looks.

Make this a reflex: before clicking anything in any crypto-related email, check for your code. No code, no click. Phishing emails are the number one delivery method for fake login pages, and this one setting turns most of them into obvious fakes. While you are in the email settings, also confirm that the email address on your account is one you actually control and monitor — a stale or shared inbox is a weak link.

Step 4: turn on the withdrawal whitelist

The withdrawal whitelist (also called address management allowlisting) is your last line of defense, and it is brutally effective. Once enabled, withdrawals can only go to wallet addresses you have pre-approved. If an attacker somehow gets full access to your account — password, 2FA and all — they still cannot send your funds to their own wallet. They would have to add their address first, and adding a new address triggers a waiting period and email notifications, which gives you time to freeze the account.

The setup is simple: go to withdrawal address management, add only the addresses you actually use (your own wallet, and any exchange accounts you regularly send to), and enable the whitelist. Yes, it adds a small delay the first time you withdraw somewhere new. That delay is the entire point. For an account holding any meaningful amount, there is no good reason to leave this off.

Step 5: manage devices and sessions

Every login leaves a trace. In your security settings you can see the devices and sessions currently signed in to your account — the device type, location, and time. Review this list once a month, and immediately after anything odd (a login alert you did not trigger, an email about a new device). If you see a device you do not recognize, remove it and change your password right away.

Related habits that cost nothing: log out on shared computers, never stay signed in on a phone you might lose without a screen lock, and keep your phone's operating system updated. Your phone is now your second factor — a phone without a lock screen is a vault with the door open. Also, turn on login notifications if they are not already on; an alert about a login from a city you have never visited is worth more than any antivirus.

Step 6: phishing links — how to spot them before you click

Phishing pages today look pixel-perfect. The tells are elsewhere:

  • The domain. Real: binance.com. Fake: binance-support.com, binance-login.net, binance.com.verify-account.io. Bookmark the real domain and use the bookmark.
  • The urgency. "Your account will be closed in 24 hours unless you verify" is a script. Real exchanges do not threaten account closure by email link.
  • The channel. Binance support does not contact you first on Telegram, WhatsApp, or Twitter DMs. Anyone who does is impersonating support.
  • The ask. Seed phrases, passwords, 2FA codes, and remote-desktop access are never legitimate requests. A real support agent has backend tools and needs none of these.
  • The ad. Scammers buy search ads on the exchange's own name. If you searched for Binance and clicked the top result, double-check the domain before logging in — or better, use your bookmark.

One more modern trap: fake apps. Only install the Binance app from the official Apple App Store or Google Play listing published by Binance, and check the developer name and review count. Sideloaded APKs from random websites are a classic way to hand your credentials to a stranger.

Step 7: API keys — only if you use them

Most beginners never need API keys, and if you do not know what they are, skip this section — you are safer without them. If you do connect a trading bot or portfolio tracker, follow three rules: restrict each key's permissions to exactly what the tool needs (read-only if it only reads), restrict it to the tool's IP addresses when possible, and never paste a key into a website, chat, or support ticket. A leaked key with withdrawal permission is as good as a leaked password, and API keys do not trigger 2FA prompts. Delete keys you no longer use; forgotten keys are forgotten holes.

What SAFU does and does not cover

Binance's SAFU fund is an emergency reserve funded from trading fees, and it has a real track record: after the 2019 hack in which roughly 7,000 BTC were taken, SAFU covered all user losses in full. That is the platform-side safety net, and it is one reason Binance is rated among the safest large exchanges in our is Binance safe analysis.

But SAFU has a boundary you must understand. It covers Binance's failures, not yours. It does not reimburse funds lost because your password leaked, because you clicked a phishing link, or because you sent crypto to a scammer's address. It does not cover market losses — if your coins drop 50%, that is the market, not a security event. And it is a company fund, not a government backstop: there is no FDIC-style guarantee behind it. The honest framing is this: Binance is among the safest places to trade, and your own account hygiene is still the deciding factor in whether your funds stay yours.

How much to keep on the exchange

Security and convenience pull in opposite directions, and the right balance depends on what the money is for. Funds you actively trade belong on the exchange — moving them back and forth for every trade wastes fees and time. Funds you plan to hold for months or years, especially once the amount would genuinely hurt to lose, belong in your own wallet, where you control the seed phrase and no exchange account can be the single point of failure. Our beginner wallet guide walks through that move step by step. A practical rule of thumb: if losing it would change your month, it should not all sit behind one password. The same logic applies on any platform you use — including Bybit, another major exchange where the identical checklist (unique password, authenticator 2FA, anti-phishing code, whitelist) applies.

The 10-minute setup checklist

  1. Unique password, stored in a password manager (or on paper, offline).
  2. Authenticator-app 2FA enabled; backup key saved offline.
  3. Anti-phishing code set; check it in every Binance email before clicking.
  4. Withdrawal whitelist enabled with only your own addresses approved.
  5. Login notifications on; current devices and sessions reviewed.
  6. Bookmark binance.com; never log in from email links, ads, or messages.
  7. Phone has a screen lock and current software updates.
  8. Long-term holdings moved to your own wallet once the amount matters.

Do these eight things and you have closed the attack paths behind the overwhelming majority of beginner losses. Security is never finished — new scams appear constantly — but the basics above are the difference between an easy target and a hard one, and attackers overwhelmingly pick easy targets.

If you think you have been compromised

Speed beats embarrassment. First, from a clean device, change your password and revoke all active sessions and API keys. Second, check recent withdrawals and your whitelist — if a withdrawal is still pending, contact support immediately; there may be a window to stop it. Third, contact Binance support through the official app or website only, and describe exactly what happened. Fourth, if funds already left, save every record (transaction IDs, addresses, timestamps, chat logs) — recovery is unlikely but not impossible, and records are what make it possible. Do not pay anyone who promises to "recover" your crypto for a fee; that is the second scam, aimed at victims of the first.

Is SMS 2FA good enough for Binance?

No. SMS codes can be intercepted through SIM-swap attacks, where a scammer convinces your carrier to move your number to their SIM. An authenticator app generates codes on your own device and is not vulnerable to that trick. Use an authenticator app as your primary 2FA method.

What is the Binance anti-phishing code and why does it matter?

It is a short word or phrase you set in your security settings. Binance then includes that code in every genuine email it sends you. If an email claiming to be from Binance lacks your code, it is fake — delete it and do not click anything in it.

What does the withdrawal whitelist do?

Once enabled, withdrawals can only go to addresses you have pre-approved. Even if someone gets into your account, they cannot send your funds to their own wallet. Adding a new address triggers a waiting period, which gives you time to react if the change was not yours.

Does SAFU protect me if my own account is hacked?

No. SAFU is an emergency fund that covers platform-side failures, and it famously covered all user losses in full after the 2019 hack. It does not cover losses caused by your own leaked password, phishing clicks, or fake support scams. Account-side security is your job.

I clicked a phishing link. What do I do first?

Act fast: change your password from a clean device, revoke all active sessions and API keys, check the withdrawal whitelist and recent withdrawals, and contact Binance support through the official app or site only. Speed matters more than embarrassment — support teams see this every day.

Should I keep all my crypto on Binance?

For active trading, keeping funds on the exchange is normal and convenient. For larger long-term holdings, move them to your own wallet where you control the seed phrase. The split most experienced users settle on is: trading money on the exchange, savings in self-custody.
