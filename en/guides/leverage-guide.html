Leverage & Futures: Understand Liquidation Before Anything Else

Leverage means borrowing to run a bigger position. At 10× leverage, a roughly 10% move against you can erase your entire margin — that's liquidation. Our position is plain: beginners should not trade futures. This page exists so you understand the risk, not as a how-to for opening trades.

Strong risk warning, up front: Futures are high-risk leveraged derivatives, and the large majority of participants lose money. Wick spikes, slippage and funding rates routinely make real losses worse than the math on paper. This article is risk education, not a trading recommendation. If you choose to try anyway, losses are yours, and only ever with money you can fully afford to lose.

What is a futures contract?

Spot means you buy the coin itself. A contract buys no coin — it's a bet on direction: go long if you expect a rise, short if you expect a fall, and every 1% move is multiplied by your leverage. The most common product is the perpetual future: no expiry date, holdable indefinitely, with funding rates keeping its price near spot (funding explained in Crypto Fees Explained).

What is leverage: one worked example

You post $1,000 of margin and open a 10× long on BTC — a $10,000 position:

  • BTC rises 5% → profit = 10,000 × 5% = $500 (50% of your margin)
  • BTC falls 5% → loss = $500 (half your margin)
  • BTC falls ~10% → loss approaches $1,000 → liquidation, margin gone

Now the uncomfortable part: BTC moving 5–10% in a day is completely ordinary. At 10× leverage, one normal day of volatility can end your position. Higher leverage doesn't demand more skill — it demands more luck. That's not a trading problem; it's arithmetic.

How liquidation happens

When unrealized losses eat so much of your margin that it can no longer support the position, the exchange force-closes it: liquidation. Two modes matter. Isolated margin: each position has its own margin bucket — a liquidation costs only that bucket. Cross margin: your whole futures balance backs every position — one bad trade can drain the account. And in violent moves, price "wicks" can spike straight through your liquidation price and snap back seconds later. The recovery doesn't matter; your position is already gone.

The 4 ways beginners blow up

  1. Max-leverage all-ins. At 50× or 100×, a 1–2% wiggle liquidates you. That's not trading; it's a coin flip with fees.
  2. No stop-loss, averaging down. Adding margin to a losing position until one wick takes everything.
  3. Overtrading. Dozens of round trips a day: fees plus funding slowly grind the account to zero while the platform profits either way.
  4. Following signal callers. The "mentor" earns from your fees and group subscriptions. Your liquidation costs him nothing. Learn to spot this pattern in Is Crypto a Scam?
Our explicit advice: finish the Beginner Path, trade spot only for at least a few months, and prove you can stay unemotional through real drawdowns before even considering a tiny futures position. Until then, anyone telling you "futures print money fast" goes straight to the block list.

If you try anyway

At your own risk, the harm-reduction rules are only four: isolated margin only, leverage at or below 3×, set the stop-loss before you open, and size positions so a full loss changes nothing about your life. Can't commit to all four? Don't place the first trade. And cost it out first with the fee calculator.

FAQ

Can leverage make me owe the exchange money?

With isolated margin, your maximum loss is that position's margin — you generally can't go negative. Cross margin can consume your entire futures balance. Exact rules vary by platform, which is why beginners who insist on trying should only ever use isolated margin.

What leverage is "safe" for beginners?

There isn't one. If a ceiling must be named: 3× or less, isolated, tiny size. Every doubling of leverage halves the distance to liquidation — ask yourself how many wrong calls in a row you can survive.

Do futures make more money than spot?

Futures are an amplifier, not an edge. They multiply gains, losses and the speed of elimination by the same factor. Outcome data consistently shows futures traders lose at far higher rates than spot holders.

What is the funding rate, and does leverage affect it?

It's a periodic payment between longs and shorts on perpetuals. The longer you hold and the larger your notional position — which leverage inflates — the more you pay or receive. Full explanation in Crypto Fees Explained.
